## 1. Foundations: Structure, System, Resource, and Transformation

### 1.1 Structure: The Medium of Energy Flow

Everybody knows that energy flows, but the hypothesis explored here is that energy always flows through structure.

Just as sound travels through air, energy flows through structure. If the universe contained no structure, there would be nothing to localize, redirect, capture, cycle, or preserve energy gradients. Energy would simply spread out toward uniformity. It would have dispersed into space long ago.


Structure: A set of elements standing in interrelation.

This is close to Bertalanffy’s definition of a “system” — but here, we reserve the word “system” for a different concept.

The simplest possible structure is a pair: (a, b), where (a, b) ≠ (b, a)

The order and grouping matter — that’s what makes this pair a structure.

Element (logical): Any thing that can be a member of a set.

Element (operational): Any thing that can be operated upon.

Thing: that which has at least one distinct, recognizable quality.

### 1.2 System

Not all structures are created equal. A rock is a structure—but it has no function. The same is true of a sand dune, or a dead tree. We can call these “inert” because they have no internal response to their own condition.

But some structures can change their condition based on their current state. A cell adjusts its metabolism based on internal conditions. A hurricane reorganizes itself based on internal pressure gradients. A thermostat changes output based on sensed temperature.

These structures enable information flow, feedback, and change. They share three consistent qualities:

1. Energy flows through them — in these types of structures, structure is inseparable from energy flow.

2. They transform one or resources to produce an effect.

3. They have a feedback mechanism that constrains their own operation.

This brings us to the definition of system:

SYSTEM: A structure that uses feedback to constrain the use of its resources when activated by energy flow.

Not all structures meet these conditions. Those that do we call systems. The question then is: what is the minimal configuration required for a system to exist?

Only certain structures can become systems. A pile of sand cannot become a system. A combustible mixture of fuel and oxygen can, if ignited.

When an inert structure becomes a system, it does so through a natural process that transforms it into a sustained, self-regulating activity. We will call this process the Genesis Cycle — for it is the foundational mechanism through which all natural systems emerge.

### 1.3 Resource, Effect, and Transformation

The first thing to understand about the Genesis Cycle is that it operates upon a resource — all systems convert one or more resources into an effect.

A resource is defined as:

Resource (R): Anything that can be transformed from one state to another to produce a consistent effect.

Effect: The outcome produced when a resource (R) is transformed.

Transform: Transformation occurs when an operation on state A causes a measurably different state B.

All systems use energy to transform one or more resources into an effect. A hurricane transforms heat and pressure gradients into sustained motion. An old truck engine transforms gasoline into controllable motion. A cell transforms nutrients into sustained metabolic activity.

The Genesis Cycle describes how a structure becomes a system.

## 4. Constraint and Freedom

### 4.1 Constraint

Constraint is defined as:

Constraint (C): An intrinsic condition or operational change in how much of a resource (R) can be transformed into a specified effect.

Because some portion of R is always constrained, R always has a condition. In FC theory, that condition is information. Information does not exist independently; it is the constrained condition of a resource. As constraint changes, the condition of R changes, and therefore the information changes.

In FC theory, the definition of “information” is: “the condition of R.”

If no resource, no constraint, then no information.

Finally, information is not created after the Origin Point. Instead, the condition of R can change to admit a greater or lesser number of distinguishable states. When there are more distinguishable conditions, informational complexity increases—and, in Shannon terms, entropy is higher. When there are fewer, both complexity and entropy are lower.

In this framework, constraint defines which conditions of R are possible. Shannon entropy measures how those conditions are distributed.

Constraint is already present at the Origin Point because the properties and current condition of R limit the range of possible transformations. We now turn to how constraint is subsequently applied to R: the Constraint Point.

### 4.2 Constraint Point

Whenever a resource is transformed into an effect, that transformation occurs under constraint.

There are only a finite number of ways any resource can be transformed, and those possibilities are determined by the properties of the resource itself. Some transformations are permitted; most are not.

### 4.3 Examples of Constraint

#### 4.3.1 Chemistry

In chemical systems, atoms interact constantly, but electron shells can only hold specific numbers of electrons. Atoms form bonds or ions only when those arrangements result in a stable configuration. Chemical transformation is therefore not open-ended; it is restricted to what atomic structure allows.

#### 4.3.2 Stars

In star formation, many gas clouds fail to ignite. Gravity pulls matter inward, increasing temperature and density, but they must reach enough mass, pressure, and temperature for fusion to begin. If they don’t, they remain gas clouds or form objects like brown dwarfs. Only a limited range of conditions allows a star to form and persist.

#### 4.3.3 Algebra

In algebra, transformations are constrained. A function or equation can only produce results that follow its rules. Algebraic transformation is therefore not open-ended; it is limited to the set of results allowed by its structure.

#### 4.3.4 Biology

In a cell, nutrients are transformed into usable energy through metabolic pathways. However, these transformations are tightly constrained. Each step requires a specific enzyme, reactions occur in a defined sequence, and only energetically favorable changes proceed. Cell metabolism is therefore a constrained flow of chemical transformation, limited to a narrow set of viable pathways.

### 4.4 The Freedom–Constraint Relationship

This relationship can be expressed mathematically.

F(C) = (R −C)/ R , where 0 < C < R

The Freedom–Constraint equation expresses the relationship between three things: the total transformational potential of a resource (R), the portion that is constrained (C), and the portion, freedom (F), that remains available for transformation.

As the equation shows, constraint and freedom have a one-for-one linear relationship within R. Every increase in constraint produces an equal decrease in the freedom remaining within R. ## 5. Accretion and Depletion

### 5.1 Resource Persistence and Energy Flow

Everybody understands that a resource must be replenished to sustain an effect. A fire burns only as long as new wood is added; when the fuel is exhausted, the process stops. For any sustained transformation to occur, there must be an accessible fuel source to replenish the existing resource as it is consumed.

To move beyond intuition, Ludwig von Bertalanffy formalized this principle in system terms, showing that open systems must sustain themselves by continuously drawing in resources from their environment.

Bertalanffy distinguished open systems from closed systems by noting that open systems exchange matter and energy with their environment, whereas closed systems, which are completely self-contained and do not interact with their environment, are idealized models. A system therefore persists only if energy and matter continue to flow through it, exchanging energy and matter with the environment.

Erwin Schrödinger identified a deeper requirement: systems persist by maintaining “order” against entropy. He showed that this is only possible if the system continuously takes in ordered resources from its environment while exporting disorder. He further noted that this order is not random, but structured—what he called an “aperiodic crystal”—a form capable of carrying specific, stable configuration. Persistence of a system, he noted, depends not just on input, but on input with the right structure.

A common source of confusion is the idea that a system “converts fuel into energy.” Energy is not created from the resource; it is either alreasdy present in the reource or it comes from outside the system.

Energy flow drives the transformation of a resource into an effect. This may include releasing stored energy in the resource(s). As the resource is transformed, energy simultaneously changes form in the interaction: chemical → heat → motion → radiation, etc. Every physical process involves energy shifting between forms (Feynman). The resource is what is being changed; the energy is what drives the change.

If energy comes from outside the syste, it may cause the energy stored in one or more resources withing the system to be released. Example: an old truck: there is potential energy in the fuel tank. There is energy stored in the battery.

Replenishing a resource from an external source is necessary—but it is not the only way a system acquires what it needs to persist. As natural systems form, the resource itself can change through interaction with surrounding elements.

In the natural world, elements do not remain isolated. They are continually brought into contact by energy flow and fundamental forces.

At any temperature above absolute zero, particles are in constant motion. Atoms and molecules move, vibrate, and collide with one another. These random motions bring elements into contact continuously, even in seemingly still environments.

Examples: In fluids, air currents mix gases in the atmosphere. Water currents bring dissolved substances and suspended particles together. Flow increases the rate and frequency of encounters. Particles naturally spread from regions of high concentration to low concentration. As they diffuse, they encounter other particles.

Example: Dust and gas in space are pulled together by gravity, leading to collisions and accumulation. This process brings particles into contact over large distances and long timescales.

Example: Charged particles attract or repel each other. Opposite charges pull together, increasing the likelihood of contact. EM forces dominate how atoms and molecules come together.

Example: Living systems actively move substances. Circulation and molecular transport mechanisms bring resources into contact.

Across all physical systems, energy flow and fundamental forces continuously bring elements into contact. These encounters are not rare events—they are constant and unavoidable.

### 5.2 Accretion and Depletion in Natural Systems

—

In natural systems:

As the resource in the Genesis Cycle comes into contact with other elements and systems, its composition can change. Elements can be added to the resource, becoming part of it, or existing elements can be lost. The resource is not fixed—it is continuously modified by nature through these interactions.

The condition of a resource—its information—determines which accretions and depletions can occur. Some elements are compatible with the resource and become incorporated. Others become incompatible with the condition of the resource and are not retained, resulting in resource loss. Accretion and depletion change the resource not randomly, but according to what its condition allows.

### 5.3 Definitions

Accretion: Increase in a resource (R) through the incorporation of compatible elements.

Depletion: Decrease in a resource (R) through the loss of elements.

### 5.4 Selective Accretion and Depletion

—

Accretion occurs because elements and resources are continually driven into interaction by energy. These encounters produce many possible elemental combinations, but only some result in accretion. The condition of the resource (the current information) constrains which additions are possible, so that only compatible elements are incorporated. Accretion is therefore selective: elements that fit the existing constraints of the resource can become part of R, while others can not.

Depletion occurs not only when resources are consumed in transformation, but also when changes in the resource alter the resource’s condition (its information). As accretion takes place (over many cycles), the condition of R changes. When that happens, some elements already in the resource may no longer fit the new condition and are lost. Depletion therefore can occur through the shedding of elements from within, as the changing condition of the resource no longer supports their retention.

### 5.5 Example: Hurricane

Example:

In the formation of a hurricane, accretion occurs as warm ocean water supplies energy to the system. Moist air is drawn inward and rises, condenses, and releases heat, reinforcing the circulation. As this process continues, more air, moisture, and energy are incorporated into the system. The structure grows and intensifies as compatible elements—heat, moisture, and airflow—are continuously added. The resource increases as the condition of the system supports further incorporation.

As a hurricane moves over land or colder water, the conditions that supported its growth change, and the system continues to use up the resource that sustains it. The supply of warm, moist air is reduced, and the system can no longer sustain itself and it begins to break down. The system loses coherence as elements are shed from the resource and it dissipates.

Accretion and depletion occur together under continuous energy flow. At every cycle of the genesis cycle, the resource is both gaining and losing elements as energy drives interactions. The changing condition of R, therefore, is the net result of these opposing processes. What persists is determined by what the condition of the resource can retain.

## 7. Energy Flow and the Waterwheel Effect

### 7.1 How Does Information Go Back Against the Flow of Energy?

Thermodynamic energy flows in one direction: toward increasing entropy. But every stable system depends on feedback—and feedback is a cycle.

That raises a fundamental question: How can information travel back against the flow of energy?

A system is a structure activated by thermodynamic energy flow. Yet for the system to regulate itself, information about its current condition must somehow affect what happens next. The feedback loop must return information to an earlier point in the process. This is true of natural systems, biological systems, and, by extension, human-made systems.

So how can a cycle exist within a one-way flow?

Consider a river. The water moves downstream. It does not reverse direction. Yet place a waterwheel in that flow and something different happens. The river continues downstream while the wheel rotates. Part of the wheel moves against the direction of the current—not because the energy flow has reversed, but because the flow itself drives a circular structure.

The same topology may explain feedback in a system.

Thermodynamic energy flows forward through the system, while that flow drives a cyclic process that returns information upstream in the flow.

The energy does not reverse. The system turns.

## 8. Thermodynamics and Information

### 8.1 The Thermodynamics-Information Theory Gap

Thermodynamics and information theory are typically treated as separate domains, each with its own models, assumptions, and methods, and they rarely intersect in a unified framework.

### 8.2 Relevant Prior Work

Several important connections between thermodynamics and information have already been established.

Ilya Prigogine showed that under continuous energy flow, ordered states can emerge and persist far from equilibrium.

Léon Brillouin linked information and thermodynamics through “negentropy." Rolf Landauer showed that destroying information has a thermodynamic cost.

### 8.3 Clash of Definitions

Thermodynamics and information theory also diverge in their use of the term “entropy.” In thermodynamics, entropy describes a one-way process: energy flows toward equilibrium, and distinctions are progressively lost. It is a monotonic trajectory—there is no reversal, only continued movement toward uniformity.

In information theory, entropy is defined differently. It measures uncertainty across a range of possible states, from complete certainty to complete randomness. This is a bipolar model, not a one-way process. Because the same term is used to describe two fundamentally different concepts, people often conflate the two, even though they operate on entirely different principles.

Identifying a direct point of intersection between these two models is therefore significant.

### 8.4 The Arrow and the Loop

This raises a fundamental problem. Thermodynamics describes a one-way flow of energy: energy moves toward equilibrium, entropy increases, and time moves forward. Nothing reverses this. Nothing moves backward. To go against it would be like trying to unwind time itself—every process is already happening, every state already lost.

And yet, information does exactly that. It loops. It compares. It feeds back on itself. Every functioning system depends on this cycle. Without it, there is no regulation, no control — no system at all.

This is the paradox. How can information circulate when the underlying flow of energy does not? How can a feedback loop exist in a universe governed by one- way thermodynamic flow? This is the fundamental tension between the two frameworks—and it is rarely addressed.

The process repeats by harnessing the flow of energy itself, as the condition of the resource (R) is updated with each turn. At the center, the condition of the resource (R) changes with each pass.
